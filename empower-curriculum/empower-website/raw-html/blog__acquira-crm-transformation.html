Case Study

We Cut Nearly $94,000 a Year From Our Software Stack

The bigger lesson was not the software savings. It was what became possible once we owned the workflow.

Hayden Miyamoto August 21, 2026 7 min read

$97K

Previous annual stack

~$3K

Current recurring cost

97%

Recurring-cost reduction

One of the companies in my portfolio is Acquira. We help people buy and sell small businesses.

Last year, we spent roughly $97,000 on the software supporting our sales and marketing workflows.

Today, the recurring cost of the system that replaced most of that stack is about $3,000 a year.

That’s approximately $94,000 in annual savings.

It’s also a 97% reduction in recurring software costs, which is the kind of number that makes you check the spreadsheet twice before telling anyone.

The old stack included Salesforce, Close, WooSender, ActiveCampaign, and Zapier. Each tool did something useful. Together, they cost us more than $8,000 a month and still didn’t give the team one reliable place to understand what was happening with a lead.

We had two CRMs, which turns out to be a very efficient way to have no single source of truth.

We didn’t start by building software

We started by mapping the workflow.

The person we asked to lead that work was Andi.

Andi joined Acquira a few years ago as a BDR and gradually took on more operational responsibility. When we put the team through our internal AI training, he was the standout.

He wasn’t a developer. But he understood the sales process because he had actually done the work, and he had unusually strong technical instincts.

We promoted him into a new role as an AI Operator. Rebuilding the CRM became one of his first projects.

Andi mapped how a lead actually moved through Acquira: intake, outreach, emails, texts, calls, follow-up, pipeline management, and reporting.

He knew where context disappeared, where ownership became unclear, and which parts of the process annoyed everyone but had somehow become accepted as normal.

That combination of operating context and technical curiosity turned out to matter more than traditional software-development experience.

The test was simple. Could anyone on the team quickly answer three questions?

  1. 1. Who owns this relationship?
  2. 2. What happened most recently?
  3. 3. What needs to happen next?

If answering those questions requires checking several platforms, searching through someone’s inbox, and opening a spreadsheet, you don’t really have a CRM. You have an archaeological project.

The goal wasn’t to replace every tool. It was to eliminate the overlap and give the team one place to coordinate the work.

We kept Airtable where it still made sense as an operational data source. We kept Gmail for relationship emails. We used Resend for campaign delivery and Twilio for calls and text messages.

Then Andi built a lightweight Acquira CRM to connect everything.

Contacts, opportunities, ownership, emails, texts, calls, tasks, campaigns, pipeline stages, and next steps now sit inside one shared relationship history.

If one person is unavailable, someone else can pick up the relationship without reconstructing it from five systems and a Slack conversation from six months ago.

This wasn’t a major development project

Andi built the Salesforce replacement in his extra time over approximately one month. We spent another month testing and improving it. The remaining pieces were added incrementally and represented another month of part-time work.

So this wasn’t three full-time months or a large outside software engagement. It was roughly three months of part-time, piece-by-piece work by a technical operator who understood the problem.

That distinction matters.

Custom software used to mean hiring a development agency, writing an enormous specification, waiting nine months, and hoping the people building it understood your business.

The economics are changing.

A sufficiently technical operator can now build a focused internal system around a real workflow without pretending to become a software company.

This is also why I think AI training needs to go beyond teaching people how to write better prompts.

The real opportunity is finding people like Andi who can combine business context, technical curiosity, and AI tools to improve how the company actually operates.

The cost difference is hard to ignore

In 2025, the systems we replaced cost approximately:

Salesforce$32,000
Close$11,000
WooSender$36,000
ActiveCampaign$12,500
Zapier$5,000

The total was just under $97,000 a year.

The current connected stack costs approximately $2,760 to $3,000 annually. That includes GitHub, Supabase, Vercel, Resend, and our recent average Twilio usage.

To be clear, the 97% figure compares recurring platform costs. It does not assign a dollar value to Andi’s time, which was well below the first year’s savings.

Even after accounting for the time spent building and testing the system, the payback is difficult to argue with.

The workflow improved too

This wasn’t just a cheaper version of the same mess.

The team now has one shared history for each relationship. Ownership is visible. Follow-up tasks are visible. Calls and messages are connected to the contact. The next person doesn’t have to start from scratch.

As campaign execution scaled, the amount of recorded outreach activity increased dramatically.

We also started tracking something we call the action-ready opportunity rate.

An opportunity is action-ready when it has a named owner, recent activity, and a documented next step.

It’s a simple metric, but it turns “the CRM needs to be cleaner” into something the team can actually manage each week.

This allowed our Account Executives to work directly in ChatGPT, create lead magnets, and send them to these opportunities every week. That effectively merged enablement, SDR, and AE roles into one.

Software savings may not be the biggest opportunity

We’re now looking at a similar situation inside another portfolio company.

The company uses ServiceTitan, Podium, and separate email software. A third-party contractor is currently building a replacement system. If the project works as expected, the recurring software savings could be around $50,000 per year.

That is meaningful, but there may be a much larger opportunity hidden inside the workflow.

For nearly two years, the company wanted ServiceTitan to let it pass credit-card processing fees to customers who chose to pay by card, while allowing customers to avoid the fee by using ACH, cash, or check.

At the company’s revenue level, that change could add approximately $15,000 to $20,000 to the bottom line every month.

ServiceTitan never made the change. ServiceTitan, to its credit, remained completely untroubled by our preferred timeline.

Because the company did not control the software, it had to wait.

The replacement is still being built, so those savings have not been realized or verified. But the new system should allow the company to make the change itself.

If it works, the roughly $50,000 in software savings may be less important than removing a limitation that could be costing the company $180,000 to $240,000 a year.

There may be a workflow like this in your business

I don’t think every company should build custom software.

Sometimes the existing tool is better, cheaper, and easier to maintain. Building your own system also means accepting responsibility for keeping it working.

But I do think every owner, CEO, COO, and department leader should take another look at the economics.

You may have a workflow that depends on several overlapping subscriptions. You may have contractors moving information between systems. You may have employees maintaining spreadsheets because the software doesn’t quite do what the business needs.

Or you may have a profitable change that has been sitting on a vendor’s roadmap for the last two years.

There could be a workflow in your stack where the cost can now be reduced by 80% to 90% without reducing the quality of the work. In some cases, owning the workflow may also let you make changes that create far more value than the software savings themselves.

If you suspect you have a workflow like this, I’d be interested in looking at it with you.

We can map how the work moves today, identify what it is really costing, and determine whether rebuilding part of it would create a meaningful return.

Sometimes the answer will be to keep the software you already have.

But sometimes you discover you’ve been spending $97,000 a year to avoid three months of part-time work.
